Review gating is fraud with a smile

Preconception

Asking only your happiest customers to leave Google reviews is just smart marketing. Everyone does it.

Everyone does do it. That is the problem. It has a friendly industry name — "review gating" — and it works like this: you text every customer a link, but the link first asks "how did we do?" Happy answers route to your public Google review page. Unhappy answers route to a private form that never touches the public internet.

Sounds harmless. Look at what it actually is: a filter that decides which customers get a public voice. Your four-star and five-star experiences get published. Your one-star experiences get absorbed. The public rating stops being a record of your work and becomes a curated highlight reel — and everybody reading it can smell that, even if they can't prove it.

The line moved and most owners haven't heard

Since late 2024, the FTC's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials made it a civil offense to use fake or misleading reviews, with penalties that can run to tens of thousands of dollars per violation. The rule targets fake reviews, undisclosed insider reviews, bought testimonials, and review suppression. Gating is not named verbatim in every clause — but selective solicitation that misrepresents the real experience of your customers is exactly the territory the rule was drawn around, and regulators have made clear that "everybody does it" is a business model, not a defense. Google's own policies discourage gating for the same reason.

The defense everyone reaches for is "but we're not making reviews up!" You don't have to make reviews up. Showing one hand of cards while hiding the other is misrepresentation all by itself. A 4.9 built by filtering is a false statement compressed into a star.

The companies selling you the filter

Here's what makes this worse than ordinary sleaze: there is an entire add-on market for it. Review-gating funnels are a checkout item — the big field service platforms bundle a "reputation management" add-on for around $39 a month that automates exactly this routing. The tooling has a compliance gloss on it and a pricing page. The tool does not care about your integrity; it cares that gated ratings are prettier and prettier ratings renew subscriptions.

The gate is a symptom of something worse

Notice what gating assumes: that your unhappy customers are a PR problem instead of an information source. That assumption is expensive. Every negative routed into a private form is a defect report you're incentivized to file and forget — and the next customer hits the same defect. Businesses that gate don't actually have fewer problems. They have the same problems with worse information and a curve that's lying to them.

Be honest — have you been asked "how did we do?" by a business, and only gotten the public link because you were happy?

Why the fearless version wins

There's a reason gated businesses fear their own customers: the filter has to run forever. The day you stop gating, the true rating surfaces, and it surfaces fast. A business with a real 4.6 and visible, answered negative reviews reads as more trustworthy than a suspicious 5.0 — because the negative reviews answered calmly are proof the business is real. Responses to bad reviews convert readers better than the bad reviews repel them. That's not a slogan; go read any competitor's page with a 5.0 and zero owner responses. It reads like a landfill of cousins' phones.

The rule of thumb. If your review process would embarrass you explained out loud in a deposition, it's not a process — it's a pending exhibit. "We asked everyone" survives a deposition. "We asked everyone, but routed the sad ones to a form" does not.

Bottom line

Review gating was a gray-area trick from an era when the rating number was the whole game. The number is now policed, the customers can smell the filter, and the machine reading your reputation summarizes patterns, not stars. Ask every customer. Fix what the unhappy ones tell you. Answer the negatives in public. It's slower, it's scarier, and it's the only approach where the number you display is a fact instead of a filter output.
