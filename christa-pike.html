Two doses and a heartbeat: Tennessee fumbled an execution

Preconception

If you can sentence a person to death, you can carry the sentence out. The state has had decades to get this right.

Whatever you believe about the death penalty — and this page is not going to settle it — hold both of these facts at once.

Fact one: in 1995, Christa Gail Pike, eighteen years old, tortured and murdered Colleen Slemmer, a fellow student at the Knoxville Job Corps center. The details of what was done to that young woman are not in dispute and are difficult to read. A court convicted Pike of first-degree murder, and Tennessee sentenced her to die. Thirty years of appeals, review after review, and every layer of the system arrived at the same order. On September 28, 2026, Governor Bill Lee denied clemency: "I am upholding the sentence of the State of Tennessee and do not plan to intervene."

Fact two: on September 30, 2026, the state of Tennessee tried to carry out that order and failed. Scheduled for 10 a.m. at Riverbend Maximum Security Institution, the execution was delayed roughly nine hours by dueling court rulings — an appeals court stay, then the Supreme Court overturning it — and the state raced the clock to proceed the same day. Two doses of pentobarbital went in. She did not lose consciousness. Witnesses heard her breathing and audibly snoring. The execution was halted, and she was loaded into an ambulance and taken to a hospital. The governor has paused all executions for the rest of the year and called for a review. A death penalty expert called it an unprecedented failure.

A state cannot hold a lawful sentence in one hand and be incapable of executing it with the other. Yet that is where we are.

The debate is real. It is also not the failure.

The arguments against this execution are serious and were made seriously: Pike was eighteen when the crime was committed, and the science on how young brains weigh consequences is settled enough that the Supreme Court already bars the death penalty for crimes committed under eighteen. Her childhood, by the state's own admission — conceded in court in August 2026 — included child sexual abuse and rape, and the institutions that should have intervened did not. Her trial lawyers were, bluntly, inadequate. Clemency petitions, documentaries, the ACLU, Amnesty International: the machinery of mercy was fully exercised, and the answer, after three decades, was still yes.

The argument for finality is equally serious. Colleen Slemmer's mother walked out of Riverbend on the 30th during a stay, having waited for this for thirty years. The court's order is not a suggestion. A legal system that cannot conclude its sentences after thirty years does not project mercy — it projects a process so entangled that the punishment exists more as theory than practice, which is its own kind of dishonesty to victims and to defendants alike.

Here is the point the debate keeps skipping: both sides needed Wednesday to work. Opponents of the death penalty needed it to work because a botched execution is the strongest argument they will ever be handed. Supporters needed it to work because a state that votes for capital punishment and then cannot perform it is either lying about the punishment or lying about its competence. The snoring witnesses of September 30 handed both camps a disaster.

The mechanism is the embarrassment

Lethal force is not actually that hard. That is the ugly truth underneath all of it. States have executed people for centuries — by rope, by rifle, by chair, by chemical. The physics of ending a life are grimly well understood. What Tennessee demonstrated on September 30 is that a modern state, with decades of notice, a thirty-year runway, unlimited legal authority, and the full resources of a government, could not reliably perform the one act its own courts ordered. Not because it is impossible. Because the state has spent that runway avoiding the hard work: secrecy over drug sourcing, protocol improvisation, litigation-shaped policy instead of engineering.

Look at the pattern across the country this decade: drugs obtained under cloak, doses guessed at, executions paused for years over what the chemicals do, then resumed with the same machinery — Oklahoma's Lockett case in 2014, where the procedure stopped mid-way; Clayton Lockett's vein collapsing on the gurney while witnesses watched for over forty minutes; Tennessee's own history of pauses and problems. And now Pike: two doses, no unconsciousness, an ambulance. If a private company failed this consistently at its one core task, it would not survive a single audit.

Responsibility cuts both ways, and that's the point

Responsibility is the word both camps use and neither owns. Pike bears responsibility for what she did to Colleen Slemmer — the abuse of her childhood explains, it does not erase, and no serious participant in this debate claims otherwise. The state bears responsibility for everything it does after the sentence: every protocol, every drug, every procedure. You cannot demand the authority and shrug off the mechanism. The moment a legislature writes capital punishment into law, it accepts a duty of competence that does not have an opt-out for hard days.

And the system bears a third responsibility nobody wants to tabulate: the decades of failure before the crime. The state that now insists on the sentence is the same state that, by its own concession, watched the abuse happen and did nothing. That doesn't decide the legal question. It should decide the posture. A state this fallible on both ends — the preventing and the punishing — has no business being casual about either.

Set the death penalty beliefs aside. Was Tennessee's September 30 execution attempt an embarrassment?

What "making sure it works" actually means

If a state keeps the death penalty on the books, it owes the sentence three things. An engineered protocol — tested, audited, transparent to the courts even if not to the press, chosen by medicine rather than by whatever a supplier will ship in secret. Institutional readiness — the ability to carry out the order the week it becomes final, not after a nine-hour legal scramble racing midnight. And ownership of the outcome — no ambiguity afterward about what was done, to whom, by whom, with what. Anything less is not a justice system executing a sentence. It is a bureaucracy improvising with a human being as the material.

By that standard, Tennessee has one honest option available. Either fix the machinery to a standard it would defend in daylight — or admit that a state which cannot competently administer a punishment has no business administering it at all. What happened on September 30 is not an argument for or against the death penalty. It is an argument that whatever you decide, you must be able to do it. We currently can't. And that, regardless of anyone's convictions about the ultimate question, is embarrassing.
