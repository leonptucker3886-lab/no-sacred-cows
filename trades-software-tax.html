The trades software tax

Preconception

Field service software is expensive because it's "enterprise-grade." If the price scales with your crew, that's just how it works.

A three-person shop gets quoted somewhere around $219 to $259 a month for the mainstream field service platforms in 2026. Take Housecall Pro's Essentials tier and add two technician users at $35 each and you land right in that range. The pitch frames this as reasonable because the software is "built for teams."

It is not reasonable. It is a tax on headcount, and the people paying it most are the ones who can least afford it: the two-truck shop trying to become a five-truck shop.

Per-tech pricing punishes exactly the wrong thing

Think about what a per-seat price actually says to a growing trades business. Every time you hire — which is the hardest, riskiest, most valuable thing a small operator ever does — your software bill goes up before the new hire swings a single hammer. Day one, zero jobs, $35 to $49 gone. If the hire flops, the software bill stays until you remember to call support.

A software bill that rises with headcount is a growth tax. The vendor has arranged things so your success is their revenue, automatically, with zero additional value delivered per hire. The tool does not work harder because you hired someone. It does not cost them more to serve your sixth tech. The price rises because it can.

Why it persists: churn and lock-in

The dirty secret of field service software is that switching is miserable. Your customers, jobs, invoices, and history live in there. Vendors know it. Low churn plus price rises plus per-seat math equals a business model where the product roadmap matters less than the renewal email. The euphemism for this in the industry is "expansion revenue." The honest word is lock-in.

And it's not like the category is expensive to serve. This is vertical SaaS — the fastest-growing corner of the software industry, now about a third of all SaaS revenue, growing at double the rate of general-purpose tools. The whole pitch of vertical software was that it serves a specific industry so well it can be lean and focused. Instead, the pricing grew enterprise while the product stayed right-sized for a truck.

What fair looks like

Fair pricing for a trades crew has three properties. Flat: one price, whole crew, no per-head meter. Short terms: six months, not an annual prepay with your hands tied. Exit doors: one-click export of your own data, because a price you can't leave isn't a price, it's a hostage situation.

Notice that none of those require the vendor to lose money. A flat $54.50 a month funds a real product for a real crew. What it doesn't fund is the sales team's renewal playbook. That's the trade the incumbents refuse to make, and it's why the math above keeps being true.

Bottom line

If you run a trades business, run your numbers tonight: seats times rate, times twelve. Then ask what changed the last time you hired. The answer for most shops is nothing — except the bill. Software that charges more for the privilege of growing your own company has confused its growth with yours. Say that out loud at renewal. It is the only sentence they have no script for.
