You're paying for AI you didn't ask for

Preconception

When software gets more expensive, it's because AI is genuinely expensive to build and run. The vendor is passing along real costs.

Some of it is real cost, sure. Most of it is a story. And the story is getting less convincing every quarter, because the numbers keep leaking out.

Here's the pattern: your software works. It has worked for years. Then at renewal, the price goes up — and in 2026 the reason on the invoice is always the same word. AI. Not "improved reliability," not "more integrations." AI. Zylo's 2026 SaaS Management Index found seventy-nine percent of IT leaders faced a price increase at renewal over the past year, median bump about eight percent, and the top named driver was AI feature bundling. Not inflation. Not compute. Bundling.

The forced upgrade

The aggressive version is what the big vendors did: make the AI SKU mandatory. You want to keep the functionality you already had? Congratulations, that functionality now lives in a more expensive tier with an AI copilot attached. You did not ask for the copilot. You may not want the copilot. It does not matter. The old tier is "legacy" now, and legacy is a synonym for expiring.

This is not a cost story. It is an upsell with a neural accent. Compute costs have been falling for years — model inference gets cheaper by the month, and every serious buyer knows it. What got expensive was the vendor's growth expectations, and that bill got forwarded to you with a sparkle emoji on it.

AI slop is a feature category now

Worse than the price is what the price buys. There is a new genre of feature that exists to be mentioned in the renewal email rather than used: the AI summary nobody reads, the chatbot that answers questions your help docs already answered, the "AI-powered insights" that restate the number three inches above it. It demos beautifully. It changes nothing. I call it slop, and the industry has started using the word too, which is how you know it landed.

To be clear about where the line is: AI features that complete real work end to end — draft the full report, route the job, answer the phone — are genuinely valuable and I will not pretend otherwise. The test is simple. Does it finish something, or does it decorate something? Decoration is what you're being billed for.

Subscription fatigue is not a vibe, it's a ledger

There's a broader weariness underneath all of this, and vendors love to dismiss it as mood. It isn't. It is arithmetic. The average small business is now running dozens of subscriptions, each one small enough to survive a glance at the card statement, each one raised annually by the same median eight percent. A thermostat subscription. A payroll platform fee. A "premium" tier on a calendar app. Individually, all reasonable. Together, a second rent.

Has software you already owned gotten more expensive this year without you getting anything you wanted?

What an honest AI price looks like

Vendors who deserve your money are doing something specific: they price the AI by the work it does, not by a percentage smothered on top of your old bill. A flat fee that includes the crew. Usage charges only where usage is real, like per completed job or per processed document. A downgrade path that actually exists. And crucially — the ability to say no to the AI and keep your price. If the answer to "can I opt out" is no, the AI was never a feature. It was a Trojan horse for the increase.

At your next renewal, ask one question. "What does this cost without the AI?" Watch what happens. If the discount is trivial or the option doesn't exist, you just learned what the product was actually worth to them — and it wasn't the value of the AI. It was the fact that you hadn't left yet.

Bottom line

You are not paying for intelligence. You are paying for a growth story the vendor can tell its investors, stapled to the product you already needed. Some AI features are worth real money — the ones that finish work. The rest is slop with a pricing department behind it. Ask what it costs without. Ask at every renewal. And when the answer is "you can't remove it," start shopping, because that vendor has stopped selling you software and started selling you the privilege of not leaving.
